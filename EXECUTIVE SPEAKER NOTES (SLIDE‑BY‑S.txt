EXECUTIVE SPEAKER NOTES (SLIDE‑BY‑SLIDE)

SLIDE 1 — TITLE
Airline On‑Time Performance & Reliability Control Tower


Today I’ll walk you through how we can improve airline on‑time performance by applying a control‑tower view to operations.
This project focuses on understanding why delays happen, where they originate, and how operations teams can intervene proactively — not reactively.

 SLIDE 2 — BUSINESS CONTEXT & OBJECTIVE
Business Problem


Airline delays are rarely caused by a single issue. They are typically the result of compounding effects,  late inbound aircraft, congested airports, and tight turnaround windows.
The challenge for leadership is not a lack of data, but a lack of clear visibility into the true drivers of delay across the network.

The objective of this work was to build a control‑tower view that identifies those drivers and enables earlier, smarter operational decisions to protect departure performance.


SLIDE 3 — DATA & ANALYTICAL APPROACH
Data & Methodology


To support this, we integrated flight operations data, airport performance metrics, and weather indicators into a single analytical view.
The focus was not advanced modelling, but operational clarity , defining the right KPIs, analysing root causes, and translating insights into management‑ready dashboards.
This ensures the outputs are actionable for operations leadership, not just analytically interesting.


 SLIDE 4 — KPI FRAMEWORK
Operational KPIs


We anchored the analysis around four operational KPIs that airline executives already understand and use.
D15 rate captures customer‑visible punctuality.
Average departure delay quantifies operational impact.
Delay propagation shows how much disruption spreads across the network.
And turnaround overruns highlight ground‑operation pressure points.
Together, these create a shared language between data teams and operations leaders.


SLIDE 5 — KEY FINDING 1
Inbound Delay Drives Departure Delays


One of the clearest findings is the strong relationship between inbound delays and subsequent departure delays.
This confirms that delays propagate across the network, they are rarely contained at a single flight or airport.
The implication is important: improving departure performance requires earlier intervention upstream, not just firefighting at the gate.


 SLIDE 6 — KEY FINDING 2
Delays Accumulate Throughout the Day


We also see a clear time of day pattern.
Morning disruptions compound as the day progresses, with the highest delays occurring in the afternoon.
This means small inefficiencies early in the day have an outsized downstream impact.
Protecting the first wave of flights is therefore one of the highest leverage actions operations can take.


SLIDE 7 — KEY FINDING 3
Airport Congestion Impacts Performance


Performance varies significantly by airport.
Major hubs consistently experience higher delays due to congestion and operational complexity.
This tells us that a one size fits all approach will not work.
Control tower prioritisation should be airport specific, with more aggressive monitoring and buffers at high‑pressure locations.


 SLIDE 8 — DECISION OPTIONS & TRADE‑OFFS
Decision Options


Based on these insights, several decision levers are available, but each comes with trade offs.
Increasing buffer time improves resilience but reduces utilisation.
Targeting high risk flights delivers focused improvements but requires sustained monitoring capability.
Improving turnaround efficiency accelerates recovery but demands investment.
The role of the control tower is to support informed trade offs, not eliminate them.


SLIDE 9 — RECOMMENDED ACTION PLAN
Recommended Actions


Bringing this together, we recommend four actions.
First, actively manage inbound delay propagation rather than accepting it as inevitable.
Second, prioritise monitoring of flights identified as high risk.
Third, focus operational improvements at congested airports where returns are highest.
Finally, rebalance afternoon schedules to absorb compounded disruption.
These actions directly target the root causes identified, rather than surface‑level symptoms.


 SLIDE 10 — BUSINESS IMPACT
Expected Impact


While exact figures would require live operational data, even modest improvements can deliver meaningful impact.
Higher D15 performance improves customer satisfaction.
Lower average delays reduce operational costs.
And improved reliability strengthens network stability.
The underlying message is that reliability is not just an operational metric  it is a competitive advantage.


 SLIDE 11 — RISKS & ASSUMPTIONS
Risks & Assumptions


This analysis is a prototype and comes with assumptions.
The data used is synthetic, weather impacts are simplified, and real‑time operational constraints were not modelled.
However, these limitations do not invalidate the insights , they underscore the opportunity once real time data is integrated.


SLIDE 12 — NEXT STEPS
Next Steps


The logical next step is to evolve this from a diagnostic dashboard into a live operational tool.
This includes real time monitoring, predictive delay modelling, and integration of crew and maintenance data.
At that point, the control tower moves from explaining what happened to actively shaping what happens next.
